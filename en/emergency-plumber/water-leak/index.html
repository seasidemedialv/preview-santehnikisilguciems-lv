How to narrow a leak down to one place

  1. A reading on paper WaterSense, a programme of the US Environmental Protection Agency, advises writing down the meter reading, using no water for two hours and checking again. If the reading has changed, something is probably leaking. Write down the cold and hot water meters separately, and you learn which line is at fault.
  2. Dye in the cistern The same programme advises dropping food colouring into the cistern. If after 10 minutes the colour appears in the toilet, the cistern leaks, and the water goes down the drain without flooding anyone.
  3. The duct and a torch In the boxed-in toilet duct, look at the joints by the meters and valves: a drip, a rust trail or a white limescale crust, which stays where water has seeped for a long time and dried.
  4. The stain and the room above it In series blocks the bathrooms are usually one above the other, so a stain on a bathroom ceiling usually leads to the bathroom a floor up, not to a room. The current building code LBN 200-21 (item 136) also provides for placing sanitary rooms above similar rooms in blocks of flats.
  5. A hiss in the silence At night, when everything is shut off, water under pressure hisses in a crack in a pipe. Thames Water explains that this sound can travel hundreds of metres along the pipe and the ground, so the place where you hear it is not always where it leaks.

Why an old meter does not show a small leak

In an LSM article a meter expert states that a class C meter registers water accurately from 15 l/⁠h, while older class A and B meters only from 60 and 30 l/⁠h. A slowly dripping joint in the duct may not move such a meter at all. According to WaterSense, a tap dripping once a second lets through more than 3,000 gallons a year, about 11,000 litres.

The class is usually written on the meter’s dial. If the test with an old meter shows nothing but the neighbour’s ceiling is damp, the search continues by traces: the duct, the bath connection, the drain.

What to write down before you fill in the form

  • Two readings with the time between them, for cold and hot water separately.
  • The meter’s class (A, B or C), if it can be read.
  • The floor, and the building’s series or number of floors, for example a five-storey series 464 block.
  • Where the stain is and how big it is in centimetres; whether it grows after a shower.
  • Whether the dye appeared in the toilet and after how many minutes.
  • Photographs: the meters, the joints in the duct, the stain.

A burst pipe or just a loose nut

Not every dripping joint means a burst pipe. WaterSafe, the plumbers' register in Great Britain, advises gently tightening a seeping compression-joint nut by a quarter turn while holding the tap body still. If it still drips after that, or the pipe itself has cracked, a new piece is needed, not a stronger tightening.

A burst pipe is usually an old steel section or a pipe in a place that freezes in winter. Rīgas namu pārvaldnieks (Riga’s municipal building manager) states that thawing a frozen water pipe is not included in the building management fee. When the pipe thaws, check that it has not split.

Questions residents ask

The meter did not change in two hours, but the neighbour has a damp ceiling. Does that prove it is not us leaking?

No. An old class A or B meter may not register a small flow, and a drain that leaks does not touch the meter at all. Check the bath and shower drain after use and look at the duct.

Who is responsible for a leak in the branch after the flat’s valve?

In an LV portāls (Latvia’s official legal information portal) explanation, the riser branches inside a flat are counted as separate property, so the flat owner is usually responsible for their faults. If the building’s documents say otherwise, ask your building manager.

Do the flat’s meters still have to be verified?

The LV portāls, citing the Consumer Rights Protection Centre (PTAC), writes that since June 2025 flats' water meters no longer have to be periodically verified. In buildings with a unified metering system, Cabinet Regulation No. 524 provides for replacing them after the manufacturer’s term or no later than 10 years after installation.
